How this deployment grew

  • 3 September 2026

    Racine Unified's school board districts join the map

    Click anywhere in Racine, Mount Pleasant, Caledonia, Sturtevant, Elmwood Park, North Bay or Wind Point and the school board card now names the Racine Unified board member who represents you, with their phone, e-mail and when their term ends. Wisconsin elects exactly two school boards by district — Milwaukee's and Racine Unified's — and until today only Milwaukee's was on the map. The card is renamed Elected School Board District, because it now answers for both. Racine Unified does not publish its district boundaries as data anywhere, and this project had recorded that as the blocker for a week. It was the wrong blocker: the districts are built out of whole voting wards, and the district's own board-election page lists every ward in every district. So the shapes are those wards joined together — 116 of them across seven municipalities — using the statewide ward map the app already carries. No picture of a map was read and nothing was traced. The result was checked against a completely different publisher: put together, the nine districts reproduce the Census Bureau's own outline of the Racine Unified School District almost exactly. One seat, District 2, is empty — the district posted a notice of vacancy in August — and the card says so, rather than quietly showing nothing, because a card that names nobody looks like a card that does not know.

  • 3 September 2026

    Every Wisconsin municipality now names its clerk

    Click anywhere in Wisconsin and the card for the municipality you are standing in now names its clerk. For a city or village that is the City or Village card; for a town — and Wisconsin has 1,239 of them, covering most of the state's land — it is the County Subdivision card, which is the only card that answers for a town at all. Either way you get the clerk — the officer who runs that municipality's elections and keeps its records — with their office phone, the municipality's own website, and the date the state's record of them was last updated. Until today those cards named somebody in nineteen municipalities, all of them in Milwaukee County; everywhere else you got a name for the place and nothing about who works there. That is 1,847 municipalities, and every one of them now has a name on it. The source is the Wisconsin Elections Commission's own statewide clerk directory, which the Commission sent when asked. It is worth being plain about what it is and is not. A clerk is not a mayor and is not a council: the card labels the clerk block separately for exactly that reason, because in 589 of these 608 municipalities the clerk is the only name on the card and an unlabelled row would read as the person who leads the place. Who governs your municipality — the council, the village board, the town board — is still not something this app can tell you outside a handful of cities, and that remains recorded in the Data gaps panel rather than quietly left blank. Two things the Commission's file contains are deliberately not shown. It carries no e-mail addresses at all — the clerks asked for those to be left out, and going and finding them somewhere else would work around a decision the people named made for themselves. And it carries street addresses that this app does not print, because the file gives no way to tell a village hall from a clerk's own house and it demonstrably contains both: several clerks serve more than one municipality and file the same single address under each of them, which is how you can tell the address belongs to the person rather than the building. Three towns out of 1,242 show no clerk, and that is not an oversight: in each case the Census and the state disagree about whether the place is still a town, and nobody publishes a clerk for the version the map draws. The card says nothing rather than guessing.\n\nEach row carries its own date because the Commission updates each municipality separately, and those dates run from 2017 to 2026. One card showing a 2017 date and another showing 2026 is the honest picture; a single 'as of today' stamped across all 608 would not have been.

  • 2 September 2026

    Iron County completes Wisconsin — all 72 counties name your supervisor

    Click anywhere in Wisconsin and the board card now names the supervisor holding your district. Iron was the last county, and with it the app covers all 72 and every one of the 1,590 supervisory seats the state files. Iron was written off here earlier today, and the reason is worth stating because it was a mistake on this side rather than anything the county did. Its board page lists all fifteen supervisors with their titles and no districts. Its full staff directory — eight hundred kilobytes, two hundred and thirteen e-mail addresses — does not contain the word district anywhere. Both of those were checked, both readings were correct, and both were about pages rather than about the county: every supervisor's OWN entry in that directory says which district they represent and which towns and wards it is made of, and the board page links all fourteen of those entries from the very names that were read off it. They were one click away, in anchors already on the page. Fourteen members give their district on their own page. The fifteenth seat is empty, and the county says so, but the vacancy is a row with no page behind it, so nothing states its number: it is district 3 because that is the only one of fifteen left over. That reasoning is only safe while the arithmetic closes, so the app refuses to publish it unless the board page shows exactly one empty seat and exactly one district is unaccounted for — otherwise a supervisor the directory had merely dropped would be published as a vacancy, telling that district it has nobody when it has someone. The districts were checked against the state's own separate record of which ward sits in which district, and they agree on all nine wards the county names and all fourteen districts. One detail is worth mentioning because it is the kind of thing that silently goes wrong: Iron names its towns plainly, without saying whether each is a town, a village or a city, and rather than assume, the app looks each name up in the state's filing and takes the type from there — which is how Hurley and Montreal are correctly treated as cities and the other twelve as towns.

    Three more counties name their supervisors — 71 of Wisconsin's 72

    Sawyer, Ashland and Douglas counties now name the supervisor holding your district, which takes Wisconsin to 71 of its 72 counties and 1,575 seats. It also closes the last hole in the covered area: Bayfield County has sat as an island on this map since it joined, because none of its neighbours named their supervisors, and all three of the counties added today border it. The covered area is now one shape. Each of the three publishes its board differently, and two of them hid the list behind a word rather than a link. Sawyer's is a staff directory of the ordinary kind, except that its supervisors' own pages state the district a second time, so every seat is checked against the county's own second telling; the towns and wards each district is made of are on a third page, and they match the state's separate record on every ward the two both name. Ashland gives each district a paragraph — the wards, then the supervisor, a phone and an e-mail — and its wards match the state's record on twenty-nine of the thirty it lists. The thirtieth is worth saying plainly rather than burying: the county's page puts City of Ashland ward 18 in District 11, and the state's filing puts it in District 8. This app decides which district you are in from the state's map, so somebody standing in that ward is told District 8 and given District 8's supervisor, while their county would say District 11. The seat is still shown, because that ward is a very small part of District 8 and everyone else in the district is unaffected — which is a different situation from Lincoln County, where the disputed ground was more than half the district and the seat is shown to nobody. Douglas publishes what is probably the clearest such page in the state and would defeat almost any automatic reader, because it writes its districts as words: 1st District, 2nd District, 21st District. Anything looking for "District 1" finds nothing at all on it. One of its 21 seats is empty and the county says so. Iron County is now the only one left. It publishes all fifteen of its supervisors by name, and the only document pairing anyone with a district is a candidate list drawn up before the April 2026 election, whose incumbent column names nine people who have since left the board. That is not a current roster, and half-using it would put six real names on the map and leave nine districts blank.

    Florence County names 11 supervisors, and the twelfth seat is empty

    Click anywhere in Florence County and the board card now names the supervisor holding your district, with a phone and an e-mail address for each. Wisconsin is at 68 of its 72 counties and 1,518 seats. One of Florence's twelve districts has nobody in it, and the card says so, because that is what the county says. The list was one link from a page this project already had on file — the county home page — which is the fifth county in a row where that was true, and it is worth being plain that the pattern is on this side rather than theirs. The empty seat is the interesting part. Florence files it as though it were a member: the list reads "Position, Vacant", and the page behind it says the name is "Vacant Position" and the district is 1. Read the way every other county's list is read, that would flip into a two-word name, pass every check that asks whether something looks like a person, and ship as District 1's supervisor — a plausible-looking name for a seat nobody holds. So the vacancy is tested before the name is assembled, and on both of the county's pages: if the list still said vacant while the member page had started naming somebody, that would mean the seat had just been filled, and the card would rather fail than tell that district it has no supervisor when it does. Two other things are worth saying. Six of the eleven e-mail addresses are personal ones — gmail, outlook, and so on — rather than county addresses. Those are what the county publishes as the way to reach those supervisors, so those are what the card shows; a rule that only accepted county addresses would have refused this county over how its supervisors take mail. And what this app cannot check here, it says rather than pretends: most counties list the towns and wards each district is made of, which is what proves that district 7 here means the same ground as district 7 on the map. Florence publishes no such list anywhere, so the district numbers rest on the two places the county does state them — the list and each member's own page, which have to agree — and on the seats running exactly one to twelve. Every member page prints a home address, and none of it is published here.

    Forest County names 17 supervisors, and says which four seats are empty

    Click anywhere in Forest County and the board card now names the supervisor holding your district, with a phone and a county e-mail address for each. Wisconsin is at 67 of its 72 counties and 1,506 seats. Four of Forest's 21 districts have nobody in them, and the card says so — that is the county's own word, printed on its own page, and it is a different thing from this app not having found a name. The page was never hard to reach. What was hard was reading it correctly, and the reason is worth explaining because it would have been invisible if it had gone wrong. The county's site gives every supervisor a card, and each card carries the supervisor's name three times over — except that two of the three are somebody else. They are the names of the supervisors either side, kept in the page so it can rearrange itself on a narrow screen. On the card for District 4, the three names are Scott Goode, Brian Piasini and Sam Augustin; only the first is District 4's. A reader that took the last name, or joined them together, would have produced a complete and entirely believable list of this board with two thirds of the seats attached to the wrong person — every name on it real, every name in the wrong place. The same leak affects the empty seats: the words "District 12 - Vacant" appear on District 11's card too, so anything that searched a card for the word would have reported three sitting supervisors as vacancies. Both are read from the card's own first heading and nothing else. Two checks ran before any of it shipped. Every filled seat has a county mailbox of the form district9@ for District 9, all seventeen, so each card states its own number twice in two different ways and both must agree. And the county lists the towns and wards each district is made of; those match the state's separate record of which ward is in which district, all 26 of them, with all 21 districts naming only municipalities the state files there. One small thing is worth saying plainly, because it is the sort of assumption that is usually invisible. Forest writes most of its towns without the word "town" — just Argonne, Hiles, Popple River — and spells out only Crandon, which is both a town and a city. Rather than assume a bare name means a town, which would have been correct here, the app looks each name up in the state's own filing and takes the type from there; a name that is genuinely two municipalities is left unresolved and fails the check rather than being guessed at. Every supervisor's home address is on that page, and none of it is published here.

    Barron County names all 29 supervisors — the list was one click away the whole time

    Click anywhere in Barron County and the board card now names the supervisor holding your district, with a phone for every one of the 29 and a county e-mail address for 28. Wisconsin is at 66 of its 72 counties and 1,485 named seats. Nothing changed at the county this week. Barron has published this list all along, and the reason it was missing here is worth being plain about, because it was a mistake on this side rather than a gap on theirs. Barron runs two websites. One is the modern county site, and its County Board page is the one this project had on file — it explains what the board does, names the chair and vice chairs, and stops there. The other is the county's older site, where its committee lists, department heads, municipal officers and meeting archives all still live, and where a page called board.cfm carries the whole thing: every district, the towns, villages, cities and wards that district is made of, the supervisor, a phone, an address to write to. The first page LINKS the second, twice, under wording that says exactly what it is — "Individual Contact Information for County Board Supervisors". A week ago this project corrected its own record about which of Barron's addresses works, put the right one in its table, and never opened the page to see whether anyone was named on it. Finding a county's board page is not the same as reading it. Two checks were run before any of this shipped, and both are about the numbers rather than the names. The county says which wards make up each district; the state keeps its own record of which district every ward is in; those two agree on all 63 wards Barron lists, and on all 29 districts' municipalities. That is what proves district 7 here means the same ground as district 7 on the map, which is the one thing a list of names cannot check about itself. And the three officers — the chair and two vice chairs — are printed twice on the page, once as a list and once as the caption under their photograph, in different wording; both are read and both must agree before either is shown. One thing is deliberately missing. The table prints a home address for 28 of the 29 supervisors, and this app does not publish home addresses, for any county. Their phone numbers and county e-mail addresses are the county's own contact details for them, and those ship.

    Lincoln County names 21 of its 22 supervisors — and says why the 22nd is missing

    Click almost anywhere in Lincoln County and the board card now names the supervisor holding your district, with the phone the county publishes. Wisconsin is at 65 of its 72 counties and 1,456 named seats, and the hole that opened in the covered area yesterday has closed. Lincoln was recorded here as a county that refuses this app, and that was true of its website and false of the county. Its site sits behind a service that asks visitors' browsers to prove they are browsers — a person never notices it; this app is turned away by it, and so is the document link that prompted this look. But the county runs a SECOND computer, at a different address, that its website never links: a public map server holding the county's own administrative layers, including one that lists all 22 supervisory districts with the supervisor's name and phone number in each. It answers plainly. Its front page is a blank server placeholder, which is why anything checking whether the address was alive would have called it nothing. Two things were checked before any of it shipped. The state's own record, published separately, says Lincoln's board seats 22 and names one of these people as its chair — so the list is not an old snapshot left on a map. They agree on ninety-eight per cent of the county. They disagree about one piece of ground — Town of Merrill ward 2, which the state files in district 21 and the county's map draws in district 9. Both agree it is that ward; they disagree about whose district it is in. This app decides which district you are in from the state's map, so on that ground it would name district 21's supervisor while the county says the seat belongs to district 9's. Rather than pick one of the two, district 21 names nobody and the card says why — which is a different thing from a seat being empty, and a different thing again from nobody publishing a name.     Menominee County names your supervisor — all seven of them

    Click anywhere in Menominee County and the board card now names the supervisor holding your district, and the two supervisors elected by the whole county besides. Wisconsin is at 63 of its 72 counties and 1,413 named seats. This is the smallest board in the state and the only one whose members are not all elected by district, which is the whole reason it is worth explaining. The county and the Town of Menominee share one governing body — the county calls it the Menominee County and Town Board of Supervisors — and it seats seven people: five for a ward each, and two elected countywide. The county lists all seven by ward, and never uses the word district. That would normally be the end of it, because this app has to know which district a supervisor holds before it can tell you they are yours. Here the state answers the question outright: Wisconsin's own record of Menominee has exactly five wards, and it files ward 1 as district 1, ward 2 as district 2, and so on to five. The county's Ward #3 is district 3 because the state says it is, not because the numbers happen to match. Each supervisor's own page was read as well, and all seven state their own name and ward exactly as the list does. The two at-large supervisors could not be filed under any district, and leaving them out would have shown a five-member board for a body that seats seven — with the vice-chair among the missing. They are named on every district card in the county instead, marked as elected at large rather than for a district, because a reader in ward 3 is represented by all three people. One thing is missing and it is the county's choice rather than an oversight: there is no phone number or e-mail address for any supervisor anywhere on the site. The only way the county offers to reach them is a contact form on each supervisor's page, and a form is not an address, so none is shown and the card links the board page where the form is a click away.

Checking each county's site for the note it leaves telling automated readers what they may visit turned up seven that ask crawlers to stay off entirely — and this app had been fetching all seven every week, five of them for boards whose supervisors it names and four for the phone numbers and office links on county officer cards. The fetching stopped and everything already read stayed, dated, because what those counties asked was that programs stop pulling their pages, not that people stop being told who represents them or how to reach them. Those cards now show the day they were read instead of implying a weekly check, and say the county asked. They will go out of date, visibly, and that is the honest price. The office contacts carry one guard the supervisor names do not need: each was recorded beside whoever held that office on the day it was captured, so if that person is replaced the contact is dropped rather than shown under their successor's name. A monthly check was added so none of this can happen again quietly, and it immediately caught a county the first pass had missed.

    Where you vote, everywhere in Wisconsin

    The ward card now names your polling place — its building, its street address, and a pin on the map at the building itself — for 7,131 of Wisconsin's 7,161 wards. Until today it could do that in two cities only. The state aggregates the pairing behind a lookup whose every route refuses automated readers, and this project had spent a fortnight measuring that refusal from every angle: each measurement was true, and each was recorded as though it described the world. It described a website. Asked directly, the Wisconsin Elections Commission sent the whole list in an afternoon. What arrived is the Commission's working copy for the 3 November election rather than its published edition, so every card says so in plain words, names the election it belongs to, dates the day it was read, keeps the state's own lookup as the way to confirm — and stops showing the pairing once that election is past, because a list of polling places that outlives its election looks current and is not. Milwaukee's and Madison's own published pairings still come first, since it is the city that runs the election. Twenty-nine wards in twelve municipalities are missing from the state's list, and their cards say so rather than guess. Building the join also turned up a fault nobody had noticed: twenty-six Wisconsin wards carry a letter in their number, and the card had been dropping it — so seven pairs of distinct Wauwatosa wards had been sharing one name on screen.

    The data-gaps panel learns where you are standing

    The panel that lists what this map cannot tell you now leads with the gaps that apply where you clicked, the way the Illinois deployment already did. It could not before, and the reason was invisible: every Wisconsin gap was recorded without the counties it applies to, and the county shapes needed to test a click against them were never built — so a reader standing on Milwaukee City Hall, having clicked, was told to click a spot on the map first. All seventy-two county shapes are now sliced from the county map this app already ships, so the two can never drift apart, and every gap names the counties it genuinely affects: the five with no fire-and-rescue filings, the fifty with no published board roster, the thirteen whose city ward filings are incomplete. Two statewide gaps are tagged to every county on purpose, because at county resolution it is better to mention a gap that does not quite reach your block than to tell you nothing is missing when something is. The misleading sentence was a fleet-wide fault and is fixed for every state at once.

    Three careful visits to locked doors, and what each one proved

    A one-shot probe had shown the state election site's refusal was about the asking environment; a follow-up expedition went to see what the site actually holds, and came back with a harder truth: the front door opens, but every interior page that might carry data — the clerk directory, the statistics pages, the election results — challenges the visitor again and stays shut, seventeen attempts across two runs. So whether the state publishes the statewide polling-place pairing as data cannot be determined from here, and the question moves from software to people: it is now an ask to the commission itself. The same expedition delivered elsewhere — Milwaukee's police-district pages proved fully readable and name each district's commanding officer, so a weekly check of those names now runs, with the captains arriving on the police-district card once the first reviewed batch lands. Every visit obeyed the same rules: a genuine browser, bounded waits, and a refusal recorded as a refusal.

    The state election site's refusal proves to be about where you ask from

The block was about the asking environment, not the site. That does not put statewide polling places on the map by itself: no bulk data file has been located yet, and access can be withdrawn as easily as it was granted, so every follow-up keeps checking rather than assuming.

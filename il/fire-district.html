Who runs my fire protection district?

Illinois fire protection district lookup — free, by address, ZIP, or a tap on the map.

← Back to the map

A fire protection district is a government of its own — its own board, its own tax levy, and no city or county above it. Where you are not inside a municipality that runs its own fire department, a district is what answers when you call 911. This names the 590 trustees and officers of the 287 Illinois districts that file an annual report with the state.

What the lookup shows

Select any point and the card names the fire protection district covering it, the people its latest filing names, and the district's office and telephone.

The map draws these districts in 26 Illinois counties. This page names the 287 districts among them that file an annual report the state publishes. A district the filings do not reach still draws on the map, and its card may still name people from its own county's data — this page is the filings, not everything the map knows.

You may not elect these trustees

A fire protection district's trustees are normally appointed by the county board, and elected only where the district's own voters chose that at a referendum. Nothing in the filings says which applies to a given district, so this page does not say it either — and it is the one board on this site where "who represents you" may have no ballot behind it. The county board that does the appointing has its own lookup.

The Role column is what tells the two kinds of name apart. A president, treasurer, secretary or trustee sits on the board; a chief, administrator, accountant or finance officer is appointed staff the board employs.

Where the names come from

Every name here is from the district's own Annual Financial Report, filed with the Illinois Comptroller, re-read on a schedule and landing as a reviewed pull request. Each filing names the fiscal year it covers, and the form has room for four names — so a board of seven can only ever file some of them, and a short list here is the form's limit rather than the whole board.

A district that crosses a county line files once, under the county it is based in, which is why one you meet in one county can be named for another. The name on each row is the one the district files under; the map's card uses whatever label the county's own boundary layer prints, which is sometimes just a village name or a number.

Not for legal or official use. Boundary and roster data come from public sources that explicitly disclaim legal precision. Confirm district assignments and officeholders with the relevant government office before relying on them for anything official.

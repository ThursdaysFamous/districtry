An Illinois park district is a government you elect and most people never vote in. It levies its own tax, runs the pools and the playing fields, and its commissioners are on the same ballot as the offices everybody has heard of. This names the 327 commissioners and officers of the 162 districts that file an annual report with the state.

What the lookup shows

Select any point and the card names the park district covering it, the people its latest filing names, and the district's office and telephone.

The map draws park districts in 18 Illinois counties. This page names the 162 districts among them that file an annual report the state publishes. A district the filings do not reach still draws on the map, and its card may still name people from its own county's data — this page is the filings, not everything the map knows.

Elected commissioners, appointed staff

Park district commissioners are elected, and a park district is its own unit of government rather than a department of the city it is named after. The Role column tells the two kinds of name apart: a president, treasurer, secretary or commissioner sits on the elected board, while a director, superintendent, accountant or finance officer is staff the board employs.

These filings name more staff than commissioners, and that is the form rather than the board: an Annual Financial Report is about money, so a district files whoever signs for it. A short board here is what the form had room for, never a claim about the board's size.

Where the names come from

Every name here is from the district's own Annual Financial Report, filed with the Illinois Comptroller, re-read on a schedule and landing as a reviewed pull request. Each filing names the fiscal year it covers, and the form has room for four names — so a board of seven can only ever file some of them, and a short list here is the form's limit rather than the whole board.

A district that crosses a county line files once, under the county it is based in, which is why one you meet in one county can be named for another. The name on each row is the one the district files under; the map's card uses whatever label the county's own boundary layer prints, which is sometimes just a village name or a number.

Not for legal or official use. Boundary and roster data come from public sources that explicitly disclaim legal precision. Confirm district assignments and officeholders with the relevant government office before relying on them for anything official.

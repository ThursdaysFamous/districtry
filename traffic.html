Notes

What the figures above can’t tell you. A small number beside a figure opens its note.
  1. What is counted. The counter counts each page, and each thing a reader does, once per browser in a visit of up to about eight hours, so reloading a page or choosing a second point adds nothing. Its own total for these two months is 56,188: pages viewed and the four kinds of event in note 10 together. The daily columns add up to 56,297, 109 apart. The gap changes from day to day and its cause is unknown, so both figures are shown. The screen chart adds up to 16,904, the browser chart to 16,906 and the system chart to 16,904, against 16,946 pages viewed, and the country chart shows only the eight with the most. The counter uses no cookies and does not identify anyone.
  2. The dates. This page covers the last two months, September 20 – November 20, 2026 (62 days), ending with the last full day. August 25 is low because Google wrongly flagged the new districtry.com address that morning, and Chrome turned visitors away before the page loaded. Those visits can’t be recovered. The Illinois school board page was viewed 467 times, the sixth most of any page.
  3. Visits by app. Older apps have had longer to be found, so read the order as age, not popularity. 236 of 16,946 pages viewed were to pages that belong to no app, such as the privacy page, so they are in no bar. A bar at zero (Colorado, New Mexico, Nebraska, Idaho, Montana, Vermont and Wyoming) means nobody visited those apps in these two months. All were on the front page for only part of these two months: Colorado (9 days), New Mexico (9 days), Nebraska (9 days), Idaho (9 days), Montana (9 days), Vermont (9 days) and Wyoming (9 days).
  4. Layers. Some layers appear in more than one state’s map, and their counts add up across states. Since September 29 each count also records which app it came from: Congress (California 15, Illinois 311 and New York 187), County (Alabama 1, Arkansas 135, Illinois 274, Indiana 53, Iowa 11, Kansas 1, Kentucky 38, Louisiana 39, Michigan 30, Minnesota 68, Mississippi 53, Missouri 33, North Carolina 74, Oklahoma 68, South Carolina 29, Tennessee 22, Virginia 10, West Virginia 1 and Wisconsin 36), County commissioner (Georgia 1, Michigan 63, Minnesota 90, Missouri 19, Oklahoma 84 and Tennessee 41), U.S. House (Arkansas 24, Indiana 163, Iowa 17, Kentucky 40, Louisiana 31, Michigan 63, Minnesota 37, Mississippi 35, Missouri 111, New Jersey 1, North Carolina 104, Ohio 1, Oklahoma 36, South Carolina 82, Tennessee 41, Texas 1, Virginia 6, West Virginia 1 and Wisconsin 59), Ward (Illinois 556 and Wisconsin 20) and ZIP code (Arkansas 17, California 8, Georgia 1, Illinois 465, Indiana 60, Iowa 11, Louisiana 17, Massachusetts 2, Michigan 22, Minnesota 23, Mississippi 32, Missouri 36, New Jersey 1, New York 45, North Carolina 42, Ohio 1, Oklahoma 14, Oregon 1, South Carolina 47, Tennessee 11, Texas 4, Virginia 10 and Wisconsin 16). “Not by app” is from before that date, or too small to be listed. The rest belong to one app only: County precinct, IL House, IL Senate and Ward precinct. Only the top ten layers are shown.
  5. Visits and sources. A visit is a page view that arrived from somewhere: a search engine, another site, a shared link, the Google Play app, or nowhere the browser would name, such as a typed address, a bookmark or an app that hides the link, which is “Direct / unknown”. Another 4,985 pages viewed came with no source at all, and they are counted as pages viewed and not as visits. That they are further pages of visits already counted, opened from a link on districtry itself, is inferred rather than read: the counter names no districtry source, and Google’s visits here match the clicks Search Console reports, which they would not if each page a reader opened next were counted as another visit from Google. Measured on October 26, they were 993 against 1,025 for August 25 to October 24, and within eight of each other on each of the 13 days from October 12, the days both record one by one. The sources chart shows the eight largest, 11,924 of the 11,961 visits in the counter’s list for this period. That list can leave out sources that sent only a few visits, so the visits figure is a floor. The campaigns list counts only links tagged for a campaign.
  6. Sources day by day. Each day is counted on its own, for page views alone. “Other search engines” is Yahoo, Ecosia, Startpage, Brave and the like, and “Other sites” any other website or shared link that sent a visitor, such as LinkedIn or a news story. The table also gives each day’s visits from the Google Play app, the visits with no source named, and the pages viewed with no source at all that note 5 infers were opened from within the site; none of these three is drawn. A number ending in + means that day’s list was cut short, so the real figure is higher.
  7. Searches. Google and Bing figures each include both districtry.com and the old chidistricts.com, which still shows up in some results and forwards to the new site. Google leaves out rare searches, so these totals won’t match its own reports exactly. The visit counter can’t see what anyone searched for, because Google doesn’t pass that along. districtry.com first appeared in Bing’s results on September 13, 2026.
  8. Shared map links. Since October 17, each Google Maps or Apple Maps link read by a search box is counted once, with which maps app it came from, whether it was pasted or shared in, what the search found, and which map read it. The link and the place are never recorded. A link the front page passes on to a state’s map is counted by both.
  9. Return visits. Each state’s map has counted a visit once per browser window since October 22, and the front page since October 29, as a first visit or a return. A visit is counted once, on the page it starts on, so a visit that starts on the front page and goes on to a map counts under the front page. A browser that has opened the front page or any of the maps before is a return; the mark that says so stays in the browser and is never sent. A cleared browser, a private window or another device counts as a first visit, and every visit before the count began was unmarked, so for its first weeks the share understates how many people come back and will rise as the count ages. The other pages are not counted here.
  10. What counts as use. Besides pages viewed, the apps send the counter events, and an event is one of four things: a reader doing something, such as choosing a point, turning on a layer or searching; the result of something a reader did, counted a second time, such as a location found or a hand-off to another state’s map; a count an app sends by itself with nobody doing anything, such as the visit counter every map has sent on opening since October 22 and the front page since October 29, or an install invitation shown; or, since October 28, a press on the short tip each map shows on a first visit or on its tour. Only the first kind is a reader action on this page. The tip’s presses are counted apart because they are what a reader did with the help rather than with the map, and each now names the state’s map it was on, while the presses counted before that are listed as “Map not recorded”. A press made inside the Google Play app is marked as well, from the same day, and the table counts those again in a row of their own. The tip being shown is not counted. The How to use page is an ordinary page and is in the pages viewed. Each is counted once a visit for each thing, so choosing five points counts one and turning on three different layers counts three. A shared link that opens on a point with layers on counts as choosing that point and turning on those layers. Until October 26 this page drew all three kinds together as “features used”, so after October 22 that chart rose while reader actions did not.
  11. The Google Play app. The Android app on Google Play opens the site with a tag of its own, so its visits are counted apart: 473 in these two months. The installed app was opened 452 times in these two months, 373 of them outside the United States.

    How the marks are counted

    1. Points chosen. A point is counted once a visit, so someone who checks three addresses in one visit counts once, and these figures are a floor. Until October 27, 2026 the counter did not say which state’s map a point was chosen on. Since then each map counts its own, which the table shows, and someone who uses two maps in one visit counts once on each.
    2. The dates. Points chosen are kept day by day, so their dates are the day the mark was reached. Visits and each map’s pages are kept only as a total for the last 30 days, so their dates are the first daily refresh that found the mark passed, and read “by”. The record begins with the figures for August 26, 2026, and a mark already passed then reads “by” too.
    3. Each map. Pages viewed counts every page under a state’s address: its map, its question pages and its county pages. It counts readers from anywhere, so someone in Indiana looking up an Illinois address counts for Illinois. A map that went live during the 30 days says how many days it has had.
    4. The marks. They were set on October 27, 2026, and are never lowered to be reached. Once the last is passed, a higher one is added.
